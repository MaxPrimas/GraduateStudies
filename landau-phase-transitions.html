Landau Theory of Phase Transitions

A system settles into the lowest point of its free energy. Change two coefficients and that lowest point can slide smoothly away from zero, or jump there all at once. Where the two behaviours meet is a tricritical point.

Temperature

t ∝ T − T₀. Negative is cold.

Quartic coefficient

Positive: continuous transition. Negative: first order.
What is being computed

Near a transition the free energy is expanded in powers of an order parameter m, keeping only the terms that the symmetry m → −m allows:

f(m) = (t/2) m² + (u/4) m⁴ + (1/6) m⁶

The m⁶ term, with its coefficient scaled to 1, keeps f bounded when u is negative. The equilibrium m is the global minimum of f. Setting f′ = 0 gives m = 0 or

m² = [ −u + √(u² − 4t) ] / 2

u > 0. The minimum leaves zero continuously at t = 0 and grows as m ≈ (−t/u)^½: a second-order transition with exponent β = ½.

u < 0. An ordered minimum appears at finite m while m = 0 is still a minimum. The two are degenerate on the first-order line, where m jumps:

t* = 3u²/16, Δm = √(3|u|/4)

Between t = 0 and t = u²/4 both minima exist and one of them is metastable. These limits of metastability are the spinodals, and they are what allow supercooling and superheating.

u = 0. The two lines meet at the tricritical point. There m = (−t)^¼, so β = ¼.

In the phase diagram the vertical axis is t and the horizontal axis is u. The filled dot on the landscape is the equilibrium state; hollow dots are metastable minima. Real examples of this structure include ³He–⁴He mixtures, where the concentration of ³He plays the role of u.
